# Relationship science papers and validated questionnaires for a relationship-analysis app ("potential" and "established" modes)

Verification note: a source counts as **verified** only if its page, abstract record or PDF was actually opened during this research. Items labelled **(not verified)** come from search-result snippets only and must not be quoted as fact without checking. ## 1. What predicts relationship quality and stability (established mode)

### Takeaway
The strongest, most replicated predictors of how satisfied someone is are their **own perceptions of the relationship**: perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction and conflict. Together they explain about 45% of the variance in current satisfaction. However, **no self-report variable predicts who will get happier or unhappier over time** (5% of variance at most). Gottman-style observational "divorce prediction" accuracy falls sharply when it is cross-validated.

### Cited Findings
- **Joel et al. (2020), PNAS (volume and issue not on the proof that was opened), "Machine learning uncovers the most robust self-report predictors of relationship quality across 43 longitudinal couples studies".** Random Forests were run on 43 dyadic longitudinal datasets from 29 labs, covering 11,196 couples — [Joel et al. 2020 PDF (UMN lab copy)](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
  - Top relationship-specific predictors: "perceived-partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived-partner satisfaction, and conflict." Top individual-difference predictors: "life satisfaction, negative affect, depression, attachment avoidance, and attachment anxiety." — [same](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
  - "relationship-specific variables predicted up to 45% of variance at baseline, and up to 18% of variance at the end of each study. Individual differences also performed well (21% and 12%, respectively)." — [same](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
  - Actor-reported variables predicted two to four times more variance than partner-reported ones. Individual differences and partner reports "had no predictive effects beyond actor-reported relationship-specific variables alone." — [same](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
  - On change: "Analyses predicting change in satisfaction were generally poor. No analyses accounted for more than 5% of the variance." Change in commitment was likewise "generally unpredictable." Data and syntax are on OSF at https://osf.io/d6ykr/. (The PDF opened is the author proof; the abstract matches the published version.) — [same](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
- **Karney & Bradbury (1995), Psychological Bulletin 118(1):3–34.** This review summarises 115 longitudinal studies covering more than 45,000 marriages. It evaluates theories of marital change and proposes an integrative model, later known as the Vulnerability-Stress-Adaptation model. The abstract reports no effect sizes. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:7644604&resultType=core&format=json)
  - The VSA model groups predictors into enduring vulnerabilities, stressful contexts and adaptive processes (source is Wikipedia, so treat as secondary) — [Wikipedia: VSA model](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulnerability-Stress-Adaptation_Model)
- **Le, Dove, Agnew, Korn & Mutso (2010), "Predicting nonmarital romantic relationship dissolution: A meta-analytic synthesis," Personal Relationships 17(3):377–390.** The paper tested interpersonal, individual-difference and social-network variables as predictors of breakup. — [Haverford repository](https://scholarship.haverford.edu/psychology_facpubs/271)
  - **(not verified)** Search snippets say it pooled 137 studies and 37,761 participants, and that commitment was among the strongest predictors. Neither figure appeared on the page opened. — [Haverford repository](https://scholarship.haverford.edu/psychology_facpubs/271)
- **Rusbult, Martz & Agnew (1998), Investment Model Scale.** In Study 3, earlier Investment Model variables predicted later dyadic adjustment and later relationship status (persisted vs. ended). Commitment is theorised to mediate the effects of satisfaction, alternatives and investments on persistence. — [Rusbult et al. 1998 PDF (Finkel lab)](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/60_RusbultMartzAgnew1998_PersonalRelationships.pdf)
- **Fletcher, Simpson & Thomas (2000), JPSP 79(6):933–940, "Ideals, perceptions, and evaluations in early relationship development".** Note that this is the ideals paper, not the PSPB paper that validated the PRQC. It followed 100 people from month 1 to month 12 of new dating relationships. — [Fletcher et al. 2000 JPSP PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)
  - At month 3, 46 of 100 relationships had ended. Month-1 PRQC scores plus ideal-perception consistency predicted month-3 breakup with a canonical correlation of .50, and "72% of participants' eventual relationship status was correctly predicted." — [same](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)
  - However, the same variables at months 1, 2 and 3 did not predict breakup at month 12, "either together or singly." — [same](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)
  - Higher consistency between ideals and perceptions of the partner predicted lower breakup. Perceived relationship quality fully mediated this effect: the correlation of −.29 dropped to non-significance (−.09 and −.05). — [same](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)
- **Gottman and the "four horsemen".**
  - **(not verified)** Gottman, Coan, Carrère & Swanson (1998), J. Marriage & Family 60(1):5–22, followed 130 newlywed couples for 6 years. Secondary sources say it predicted divorce from observed conflict affect with more than 80% accuracy. Only snippets were seen. — [PMC4298140 (snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4298140); [Gottman Institute blog (practitioner source)](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-four-horsemen-recognizing)
  - **Heyman & Smith Slep (2001), "The Hazards of Predicting Divorce Without Crossvalidation," J. Marriage & Family 63(2):473–479.** The authors re-analysed archival divorce-prediction data, using Gottman et al. (1998) as the main example, and report that "accuracy and predictive value drops precipitously during crossvalidation." They argue that studies without cross-validation should be read with great caution. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC1622921&resultType=core&format=json)
  - **(not verified)** Stanley, Bradbury & Markman (2000), "Structural flaws in the bridge from basic research on marriage to interventions for couples," J. Marriage & Family 62(1), critiqued Gottman et al. (1998), and Gottman et al. (2000) replied. — [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/docview/219750581)
- **NLP detection of conflict behaviours in couples.**
  - **(not verified)** USC SAIL's "behavioral signal processing" work (Black, Georgiou, Narayanan et al., 2010–2013) automatically predicted human-coded couple-therapy behaviours from speech acoustics and ASR-derived lexical features. The codes were blame, acceptance, humor, positive/negative affect and sadness, in 117–134 distressed couples. No accuracy figures were verified. — [ISI: lexical features paper](https://www.isi.edu/results/publications/17485/thats-aggravating-very-aggravating-is-it-possible-to-classify-behaviors-in-couple-interactions-using-automatically-derived-lexical-features/); [Interspeech 2010](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2010/black10_interspeech.pdf); [USC ICT "You made me do it"](https://ict.usc.edu/pubs/You%20made%20me%20do%20it.pdf)

### Inferences
- In established mode, the most evidence-based inputs are the user's **own ratings** of perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction and conflict. A short self-report check-in is likely to carry more signal than chat-log features. This inference rests on Joel 2020 showing that actor reports dominate.
- The app should **not** claim to forecast whether a relationship will improve, worsen or end. Joel 2020 found change essentially unpredictable (5% of variance at most). Fletcher 2000's 72% short-horizon breakup classification did not hold at 12 months. Heyman & Slep 2001 show that Gottman-type accuracy collapses under cross-validation.
- The four horsemen are best used as **descriptive feedback categories** (for example, "this message reads as criticism rather than a complaint") rather than predictors. A text classifier for them would need custom labelling, since no validated text dataset was found. The USC behavioural-coding work is the closest academic precedent.

### Gaps
- No full text of Le et al. 2010 was opened, so its per-predictor effect sizes (commitment, love, alternatives, etc.) are unverified.
- No primary text of Gottman 1998 was opened, so its exact accuracy figures and the contempt and defensiveness coefficients are unverified.
- No accuracy figures were verified for the USC lexical blame classifiers.
- I found no peer-reviewed NLP paper or dataset that labels text with Gottman's four horsemen (criticism, contempt, defensiveness, stonewalling). The search surfaced only practitioner pages and blogs.

## 2. What signals romantic interest early on (potential mode)

### Takeaway
Evidence-based early-interest signals include **reciprocal self-disclosure**, the timing of follow-up contact, and perceived reciprocity and reliability. The effects come mostly from lab, vignette or self-report studies, not from naturalistic chat logs. Breadcrumbing (intermittent, non-committal contact) is associated with worse wellbeing in the person receiving it.

### Cited Findings
- **Collins & Miller (1994), "Self-disclosure and liking: a meta-analytic review," Psychological Bulletin 116(3):457–475.** The meta-analysis tested three effects: (1) people who disclose intimately are liked more; (2) people disclose more to those they already like; (3) people like others more after disclosing to them. "Significant disclosure-liking relations were found for each effect." Study paradigm, disclosure type and the discloser's gender moderated the effects. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:7809308&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Teichmann, Petrowsky, Boecker, Soliman & Loschelder (2026), "How the timing of texting triggers romantic interest after the first date: A curvilinear U-shaped effect and its underlying mechanisms," J. Social & Personal Relationships 43(2):570–593, DOI 10.1177/02654075251377184.** — [Leuphana research portal](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/how-the-timing-of-texting-triggers-romantic-interest-after-the-fi/)
  - Design: a pre-study (N = 100) set the conditions as immediately after the date, the next morning, or after two days. The main experiment had N = 543.
  - Results: the effect was curvilinear, with **next-morning** texting producing the highest relationship intentions. Women were more sensitive to timing than men.
  - Mechanism: mediation ran through **perceived reciprocity and perceived reliability**.
  - Caveat: participants imagined a neutral first date in a vignette; it was not a real date. — [same](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/how-the-timing-of-texting-triggers-romantic-interest-after-the-fi/)
- **Navarro, Larrañaga, Yubero & Víllora (2020), "Psychological Correlates of Ghosting and Breadcrumbing Experiences," IJERPH 17(3):1116.** Sample: N = 626 adults aged 18–40. Experiencing breadcrumbing (alone or together with ghosting) was associated with lower life satisfaction and higher helplessness and loneliness. Ghosting alone had no significant association with any of the three measures. The abstract gives no effect sizes. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:32050561&resultType=core&format=json)
  - **(not verified)** A companion paper by the same group reports that about 2 in 10 participants experienced or initiated ghosting, and just over 3 in 10 experienced or initiated breadcrumbing, in the past 12 months. Dating-app use and online surveillance raised the likelihood of both. — [Escritos de Psicología](https://revistas.uma.es/index.php/espsi/article/download/9960/13115?inline=1)
- **Reciprocity of liking and perceived similarity.**
  - **(not verified)** Reciprocity of liking is cited as a major predictor of attraction (Montoya & Horton 2012; Eastwick & Finkel 2009). — [Springer, Current Psychology 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-021-01466-4)
  - **(not verified)** Montoya, Horton & Kirchner (2008) found that similarity-attraction effects are driven mainly by perceived rather than actual similarity. — [Typeset copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/is-actual-similarity-necessary-for-attraction-a-meta-1ws2438nl3.pdf)
  - **(not verified)** The "liking gap" (Boothby et al. 2018): people underestimate how much a new conversation partner likes them. — [Springer, Current Psychology 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-021-01466-4)
- **Misperception of interest.** **(not verified)** Haselton & Buss's error-management theory predicts that men overperceive women's sexual interest. Later work found that this sex difference shrank or disappeared once men's own interest (projection) and sociosexuality were controlled. — [Buss lab: Misperception of Sexual Interest](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/buss/files/2015/09/The-Misperception-of-Sexual-Interest.pdf)
- **App reply-rate data.** **(not verified, industry data)** Hinge reportedly analysed 300,000 conversations. The chance of a reply to a single message fell from 55% to 38% within the first half hour, then declined more gradually over the following hours. — [Reader's Digest](https://www.rd.com/article/wait-response-text-message/)

### Inferences
- For potential mode, the most defensible features are:
  - **Disclosure reciprocity**: whether the other person matches the depth of the user's self-disclosures (Collins & Miller 1994).
  - **Initiation and reply balance, and consistency**, framed as perceived reciprocity and reliability (Teichmann 2026's mediators).
  - **Breadcrumbing patterns**: intermittent contact with no follow-through.
- Users who are interested may **project** their own interest onto the other person (error-management literature, not verified). The app should present signals as uncertain and avoid "they like you X%" scores.
- I found no peer-reviewed study validating double-texting, emoji counts or reply latency as markers of interest in real chats. These should be labelled heuristic or experimental features.

### Gaps
- I did not verify any naturalistic study linking reply latency, double-texting, initiation ratio or emoji use in real chat logs to mutual interest.
- The Montoya reciprocal-liking meta-analysis and Reis's responsiveness-in-initial-interactions work (for example Birnbaum & Reis 2012) were not opened.
- No ghosting prevalence figure was verified from a primary page. The two Navarro papers report different rates (2020 IJERPH table: ghosting 4.8%, breadcrumbing 14.1%, from a snippet).

## 3. Attachment style from language or behaviour, and its effect on perceived interest

### Takeaway
Attachment anxiety and avoidance track messaging behaviour in theory-consistent ways. Anxious people want more messages and reply faster; avoidant people send fewer messages and reply more slowly. Inferring attachment from text is still at the research-prototype stage, so the app should measure attachment with the ECR-R rather than infer it from chats.

### Cited Findings
- **Patel, Mahar & Webster (2022), "Adult Romantic Attachment, Electronic Messaging, and Relationship Quality," Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 25(3):200–206.** Sample: N = 302 romantically partnered undergraduates. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35085449&resultType=core&format=json)
  - Anxious attachment: wanting more messages than received, and receiving fewer messages from partners.
  - Avoidant attachment: wanting fewer messages than received, and sending fewer messages.
  - Reply speed: anxious people replied to partners faster than partners replied to them; avoidant people replied more slowly.
  - The link between satisfaction with message frequency and relationship quality was stronger for more anxious people.
  - The abstract gives no effect sizes. — [same](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35085449&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Soares, McCurdy, Gerber & Fonagy (2024), "Chatting Up Attachment: Using LLMs to Predict Adult Bonds," arXiv:2409.00347.**
  - Data: GPT-4 and Claude 3 Opus simulated Adult Attachment Interviews. Models were evaluated on 9 real human transcripts labelled by clinicians.
  - Results: training on synthetic data only performed comparably to training on human data. Synthetic embeddings sat in a different region from human ones until standardisation was applied.
  - The abstract gives no numeric accuracy. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00347v1)
- **(not verified)** Older text-based work:
  - Pronoun and emotion-word patterns in couples' interviews classified attachment style in about 50% of cases, or about 58% against a questionnaire criterion — [UT repository](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2152/980?show=full)
  - An SVM on autobiographical texts from 202 participants classified attachment at 64–85%, depending on style — [Computación y Sistemas](https://unpaywall.org/10.13053%2Fcys-22-4-3064)
- **(not verified)** Weisskirch (2012) found attachment anxiety associated with more texts sent and received (N = 31 women). Drouin & Landgraff (2012) found avoidance associated with less texting to partners. — [bepress: Weisskirch](https://works.bepress.com/rob-weisskirch/28); [Psychology Today summary](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-freedom-change/201808/why-attachment-styles-and-texting-dont-always-mix)
- **Joel et al. 2020:** attachment avoidance and attachment anxiety were among the top five individual-difference predictors of relationship quality. — [Joel et al. 2020 PDF](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
- **ECR-R (Fraley, Waller & Brennan 2000):** 36 items, with 18 for Anxiety and 18 for Avoidance. Internal consistency is typically .90 or higher. IRT analyses show reliability is somewhat lower at the secure end of both dimensions. — [Fraley ECR-R page](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/ecrr.htm)

### Inferences
- Reply-speed and message-frequency gaps between partners may partly reflect **attachment differences rather than interest**: an avoidant person replies slowly, and an anxious person over-reads that delay. The app could ask the user to complete the ECR-R and then calibrate its interpretation, for example: "your anxiety score is high; slow replies may feel more alarming than they are."
- Text-based attachment inference is not validated on casual chat. If it is offered, it should be labelled experimental and never shown as a diagnosis.

### Gaps
- No verified study shows how attachment anxiety biases the **perception of a potential partner's interest** specifically.
- No validated model predicts ECR-R scores from chat or DM text.
- I found no social-media attachment dataset.

## 4. Text-based detection of unhealthy dynamics (coercive control, digital dating abuse, gaslighting, monitoring)

### Takeaway
Validated self-report instruments exist for digital and cyber dating abuse (the CDAQ, and Reed et al.'s DDA measure). Monitoring and control behaviours are very common among young couples. NLP resources for manipulation exist (MentalManip, CC BY 4.0), but they are built from movie dialogue, and the authors report that models struggle with them. Nothing is validated for detecting gaslighting or coercive control in real couples' chats.

### Cited Findings
- **Borrajo, Gámez-Guadix, Pereda & Calvete (2015), "The development and validation of the Cyber Dating Abuse Questionnaire among young couples," Computers in Human Behavior 48:358–365, DOI 10.1016/j.chb.2015.01.063.** — [Universitat de Barcelona repository](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/8715a427-3b10-4cf3-8c34-40f50cd63e7a/full)
  - Sample: 788 people aged 18–30 (77.3% women, mean age 22.72).
  - Structure: exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis supported two factors, **direct aggression** (insults, threats) and **monitoring/control** (for example, using a partner's passwords), for both victimisation and perpetration.
  - Reliability: Cronbach's alpha was described as "adequate".
  - Prevalence: direct aggression above 10%; monitoring/control above 70%.
  - Licence: the repository postprint is CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. This licenses the article; it says nothing about the questionnaire items. — [same](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/8715a427-3b10-4cf3-8c34-40f50cd63e7a/full)
  - **(not verified)** One snippet says the CDAQ has 20 items rated on a 6-point scale. — [UB GREVIA](https://www.ub.edu/grevia/tag/cdaq/)
- **(not verified) Reed, Tolman & Ward (2016) Digital Dating Abuse measure.** It has three subscales: digital monitoring and control, digital direct aggression, and digital sexual coercion. In 703 Midwestern high-school students with dating experience, 53.8% reported monitoring and control, 46.3% direct aggression and 32.2% sexual coercion (Reed et al. 2017). A 2018 critical review (Brown & Hegarty) says no DDA measure is the gold standard. — [PMC7993642 (snippet)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7993642); [Reed dissertation, Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/113629/reedla_1.pdf); [Univ. of Melbourne record of the critical review](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1326122-digital-dating-abuse-measures--a-critical-review)
- **Smith et al. (2015), "Taking Stock of Behavioral Measures of Adolescent Dating Violence," J. Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma 24(5):674–692.** The review identified 48 behavioural measures of adolescent dating violence. The most common were the CTS-2, the Safe Dates Scale and the CADRI. — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC5875428&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Wang, Yang, Hassanpour & Vosoughi (2024), "MentalManip: A Dataset For Fine-grained Analysis of Mental Manipulation in Conversations," ACL 2024, arXiv:2405.16584.** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16584v1)
  - Data: 4,000 annotated movie dialogues, labelled for manipulation techniques and the victim vulnerabilities targeted.
  - Results: leading models struggled to recognise manipulative dialogue, and fine-tuning on mental-health or toxicity datasets did not fix this.
  - Licence: CC BY 4.0. — [same](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16584v1)
  - **(not verified)** A 2025 follow-up dataset called REAMENT uses 5,000 real-world dialogues curated from YTD-18M. — [arXiv 2505.15255](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.15255v2)
  - **(not verified)** "Can a Large Language Model be a Gaslighter?" (2024) studies LLMs *producing* gaslighting; it is not a detection dataset. — [arXiv 2410.09181](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.09181v1)

### Inferences
- A safety feature should combine (a) a **validated self-report screen** adapted from CDAQ or DDA items covering monitoring, control and direct aggression, with (b) **text flags** for explicit patterns such as password demands, location-tracking demands, threats and insults. Flags should come with resources rather than verdicts.
- Because monitoring and control is so common (above 70% in the CDAQ sample), the app should frame these behaviours by frequency and impact, not as automatic proof of abuse.
- MentalManip (CC BY 4.0) could serve as auxiliary training data for manipulation detection, but transfer to real romantic chat is unproven.

### Gaps
- No validated text classifier for coercive control or gaslighting in intimate-partner messages was found.
- The CDAQ item wording, per-subscale alphas and its terms of use for commercial apps were not verified.
- I did not verify any licence for the Reed DDA items.

## 5. Validated scales: items, scoring, reliability, licensing

### Takeaway
The most app-friendly measures are:
- **CSI-4**, for brief satisfaction tracking. It is "freely available for use", and the paper gives a distress cut score.
- **PRQC**, 18 items covering six components.
- The **Investment Model Scale**. Its commitment subscale works on its own, and the authors call commitment arguably an excellent single indicator of overall couple adjustment.
- **ECR-R**, for attachment.
- **PPRS/PRIS**, for responsiveness.

CSI, RAS, PRQC and IMS commitment measure relationship quality itself, which is the **outcome** in Joel 2020. They do not measure that study's top predictors.

**Every scale whose terms were found allows research or clinical use, but commercial use needs author permission or is unstated.** A commercial app should contact the authors.

### Cited Findings
- **Funk & Rogge (2007), J. Family Psychology 21(4):572–583, "Testing the Ruler With Item Response Theory".** Sample: N = 5,315 online respondents (80% female, mean age 26). The CSI scales showed more measurement precision than the MAT and DAS, and therefore more power to detect differences. — [Funk & Rogge 2007 PDF](https://www.courses.rochester.edu/surveys/funk/Funk%20%26%20Rogge%202007.pdf)
  - In Table 1, distress cut scores were derived with ROC analysis against the DAS cut of 97.5, using the 1,092 respondents who fell below it. — [same](https://www.courses.rochester.edu/surveys/funk/Funk%20%26%20Rogge%202007.pdf)
  - Rogge's lab page lists CSI-32, CSI-16 and CSI-4 with downloadable .docx files and says the scales are "freely available for use." It says nothing about commercial use. — [Rogge lab Research Tools](https://www.courses.rochester.edu/surveys/funk/main.htm)
- **Reis lab page:** both PPRS and PRIS carry the statement "Permission is hereby granted for use in clinical and research settings. For commercial usage, please contact the authors." — [Reis lab Research Measures](https://sites.rochester.edu/reis/research-measures/)
- **Fraley's ECR-R page (paraphrased):** the scales were published for public use; no author permission is needed for non-commercial research, but commercial use requires permission. Scoring: reverse-key the starred items (8 minus the response) and average each subscale. — [Fraley ECR-R page](https://labs.psychology.illinois.edu/~rcfraley/measures/ecrr.htm)
- **Rusbult et al. 1998 Appendix (IMS):**
  - Satisfaction, Alternatives and Investment each have one block of facet items followed by 5 global items. Facet items have four labelled options: Don't Agree At All, Agree Slightly, Agree Moderately, Agree Completely. No numeric values are printed for them.
  - Commitment has 7 items.
  - Global items are rated 0–8 ("Do Not Agree At All" / "Agree Somewhat" / "Agree Completely").
  - Commitment alpha was .91, .91 and .95 across Studies 1–3.
  - The authors' advice is to "administer both facet and global items; analyze only global items."
  - Without the facet items, alphas for the global measures of the three bases of dependence "tend to range from .55 to .85."
  - "It is entirely appropriate to utilize the commitment subscale on its own." The authors call commitment "arguably ... an excellent single indicator of overall couple adjustment." — [Rusbult et al. 1998 PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/60_RusbultMartzAgnew1998_PersonalRelationships.pdf)
- **PRQC (Fletcher, Simpson & Thomas 2000, PSPB):** six components (Love, Passion, Commitment, Trust, Satisfaction, Closeness), each with 3 items on a 7-point scale. A confirmatory factor analysis supported six first-order factors loading on one second-order quality factor. This description comes from the companion JPSP paper, which was opened; the PSPB validation paper itself was not. — [Fletcher et al. 2000 JPSP PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Recommended stack.**
  - Established mode: CSI-4 for weekly or monthly tracking, plus selected PRQC items and the IMS commitment items. These are **outcome** measures of quality and commitment.
  - To capture Joel 2020's top *predictors* (perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, conflict), the app needs separate items. No validated scale for them was identified in this research.
  - Both modes: the ECR-R, taken once.
  - Responsiveness: PPRS/PRIS items.
  - Safety: a short screen adapted from the CDAQ or DDA measure.
- The CSI-4 and RAS cut scores come from a single convenience sample (online, 80% female) and were calibrated against the DAS. Present them as "in the range that researchers associate with relationship distress", not as a diagnosis.
- Using these scales inside a commercial app requires emailing the authors: Rogge (CSI), Reis (PPRS/PRIS), Fraley (ECR-R), Agnew (IMS), Hendrick (RAS), Fletcher (PRQC).

### Gaps
- No validated items were identified for Joel 2020's top predictors: perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction and conflict. Joel's OSF master predictor list (https://osf.io/d6ykr/) was not opened.
- No licence statement was found for the PRQC, IMS, TLS or RAS from an authoritative source.
- PPRS and PRIS item counts and alphas were not verified from the primary papers.
- CDAQ item wording was not opened.
- The PSPB PRQC validation paper (Fletcher, Simpson & Thomas 2000, PSPB 26:340–354; volume and pages from memory, not verified) was not opened, so per-component alphas are missing.

## 6. Effect sizes and how predictive these measures are (avoiding overclaiming)

### Takeaway
Current relationship satisfaction is moderately to highly predictable from one's own perceptions (up to 45% of variance). Future satisfaction is less predictable (up to 18%). **Change is essentially unpredictable (5% at most).**

Breakup prediction is weaker than it looks:
- Fletcher 2000 classified 3-month breakup at 72% in-sample, but nothing predicted breakup at 12 months.
- Gottman 1998's reported accuracy of more than 80% is not verified. Heyman & Slep 2001 show that accuracy of this kind drops precipitously under cross-validation. The app should show descriptive feedback and uncertainty, not forecasts.

### Cited Findings
- Up to 45% of variance at baseline and 18% at the end of each study from relationship-specific variables. Individual differences: 21% and 12%. Change in satisfaction: no analysis exceeded 5% of variance. — [Joel et al. 2020 PDF](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
- Partner reports added nothing beyond the user's own reports. — [same](https://socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/sites/socialinteractionlab.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-12/joel_et_al._pnas_2020.pdf)
- Early-dating breakup within 3 months: 72% correctly classified (canonical r = .50) from month-1 measures in N = 100. Whether the paper cross-validated this was not checked. Nothing predicted breakup at 12 months. — [Fletcher et al. 2000 JPSP PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/sociology/2000-fletcher.pdf)
- Divorce-prediction "accuracy and predictive value drops precipitously during crossvalidation." — [Heyman & Smith Slep 2001 (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC1622921&resultType=core&format=json)
- Scale intercorrelations in Funk & Rogge 2007 are very high: the CSI forms correlate .84–.98 with the other eight satisfaction scales, and .97–.99 with each other. Different satisfaction scales are therefore largely interchangeable, and the CSI's advantage is precision rather than a different construct. — [Funk & Rogge 2007 PDF](https://www.courses.rochester.edu/surveys/funk/Funk%20%26%20Rogge%202007.pdf)
- Several key texting and ghosting studies report associations without effect sizes in their abstracts (Patel 2022; Navarro 2020). Teichmann 2026 is a vignette experiment. — [Patel (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35085449&resultType=core&format=json); [Navarro (Europe PMC)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:32050561&resultType=core&format=json); [Teichmann (Leuphana)](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/how-the-timing-of-texting-triggers-romantic-interest-after-the-fi/)
- MentalManip authors: state-of-the-art models struggle to detect manipulation in dialogue. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16584v1)

### Inferences
- **Messaging guidance:**
  - Say: "people who rate X highly tend to be more satisfied."
  - Do not say: "your relationship has a 73% chance of lasting."
  - Show uncertainty ranges, and say that change over time is hard to predict even for researchers (Joel 2020).
- Chat-log features have not been shown to add predictive power beyond self-report for relationship quality. Joel 2020 used self-report only, and chat features were not studied there. Treat text-derived scores as exploratory.
- Single-sample cut scores (CSI-4 13.5, RAS 23.5) should be labelled as research thresholds.

### Gaps
- I found no verified effect sizes for the individual predictors in Le et al. 2010, or for Gottman's specific behaviours (contempt, stonewalling).
- No study was found that compares the predictive power of chat-log features with self-report scales for relationship outcomes.
