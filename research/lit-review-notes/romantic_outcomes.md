# Predicting romantic relationship status and outcomes from exchanged language (texts, IM, speed-date talk, dating messages, social media)

Verification legend used below:
- **[V-abstract]**: I read the paper's abstract and the numbers come from it. The source was the Europe PMC REST API (`ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search`, queried by DOI), Crossref, the arXiv or AIS abstract page, or an author page. Where a link below points to doi.org, the abstract text itself came from the Europe PMC API.
- **[V-fulltext]**: I opened the full text (a PDF or the PMC article) and the numbers come from it.
- **[V-biblio]**: the bibliographic record is confirmed, but I could not open the abstract or full text. Findings come from secondary sources and are marked as such.
- "not verified": the figure was not on any page I opened.

## Q1. Which real, verifiable studies predict romantic interest, initiation, stability or breakup, or satisfaction from the language two people exchange, and what did each find?

### Takeaway
The core, well-verified evidence base is small and mostly old (2006–2014). Dyadic language features, such as language style matching (LSM), emotion words, dialogue acts and prosody, predict mutual romantic interest after speed dates, staying together 3 months later in IM couples, and first dates in online dating. The effects are moderate (odds ratios of about 2–3; classifier accuracy of about 60–75% against a 50% baseline). In two general web searches I found **no peer-reviewed 2022–2025 ML or LLM study that predicts breakup or relationship quality from couples' donated chat logs**. I did not query Google Scholar, the ACL Anthology or arXiv directly, so this is an absence of findings, not evidence that no such study exists. Recent donated-message work, such as Brinberg & Ram 2021 on iMessage, is descriptive and has not yet tested outcomes.

### Cited Findings

#### 1. Ireland, Slatcher, Eastwick, Scissors, Finkel & Pennebaker (2011) [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Ireland ME, Slatcher RB, Eastwick PW, Scissors LE, Finkel EJ, Pennebaker JW. (2011). Language style matching predicts relationship initiation and stability. *Psychological Science*, 22, 39–44 (issue number not verified). DOI 10.1177/0956797610392928. PMID 21149854. — [Europe PMC record / abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21149854); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610392928)
- **Data:** Study 1 used transcripts of **40 speed dates**. Study 2 used instant messages from **86 dating couples**. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21149854)
- **Outcomes:** Study 1: mutual romantic interest, meaning both people wanted future contact. Study 2: still dating at a **3-month follow-up**. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21149854)
- **Method:** Language style matching, meaning dyad similarity in function-word use, entered as a predictor of binary outcomes. The abstract reports odds ratios. That the function words were counted with LIWC and that the models were logistic is my inference (it is standard for this group's work) and was not verified on the page. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21149854)
- **Headline results:** Study 1: LSM predicted mutual romantic interest, **OR = 3.05**. **33.3%** of pairs above the median LSM mutually desired contact, against **9.1%** at or below the median. Study 2: LSM in IMs predicted stability, **OR = 1.95**. **76.7%** of couples above the median LSM were still dating at follow-up, against **53.5%** at or below it. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21149854)
- **Limitations (my reading of the abstract):** The samples are very small (40 dates, 86 couples). The predictor is a single feature. There is no out-of-sample prediction or cross-validation. Study 2 uses the same 86-couple IM dataset as Slatcher & Pennebaker 2006 (both report 86 dating couples and IM collection with a 3-month follow-up). The abstract does not state this overlap explicitly, so treat it as inferred.
- **Data availability:** not verified. No data statement appears in the abstract.

#### 2. Slatcher & Pennebaker (2006) [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Slatcher RB, Pennebaker JW. (2006). How do I love thee? Let me count the words: the social effects of expressive writing. *Psychological Science*, 17, 660–664 (issue number not verified). DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01762.x. Abstract read via the Europe PMC REST API title search. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01762.x)
- **Data:** **86 dating couples**. One partner per couple was randomized to 3 days of expressive writing about the relationship or a control condition (writing about daily activities). **Instant messages** were collected from the couples in the days before and after the writing. — [DOI / abstract via Europe PMC API](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01762.x)
- **Outcome:** Still dating the partner **3 months later**.
- **Method:** Experimental manipulation plus LIWC-style word counts of the IMs, and a mediation analysis.
- **Headline result:** People who wrote about their relationship were "significantly more likely" to still be dating 3 months later. Both partners used more positive and negative emotion words in their IMs after relationship writing. **Increases in positive-emotion words partially mediated** the effect of writing on stability. The exact percentages and effect sizes are not in the abstract (not verified). — [DOI / abstract](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01762.x)
- **Limitations:** Small sample. The primary manipulation is the writing task, so this is not a pure prediction study. Language enters only as a mediator.
- **Data availability:** not verified.
- **DOI note:** the correct DOI ends in **.01762.x**. A neighboring DOI, .01782.x, belongs to an unrelated infant visual-memory paper (Oakes et al. 2006), so check this when citing.

#### 3. Ranganath, Jurafsky & McFarland (2009), EMNLP [V-fulltext: PDF text extracted and searched]
- **Citation:** Ranganath R, Jurafsky D, McFarland D. (2009). It's Not You, it's Me: Detecting Flirting and its Misperception in Speed-Dates. *Proceedings of EMNLP 2009*, pp. 334–342, Singapore. ACL. No DOI; Anthology ID D09-1035. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1035/); [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1035.pdf)
- **Data:** The SpeedDate corpus, described in Jurafsky et al. 2009. It comes from 3 speed-dating sessions at an American university in 2005 with graduate-student participants. About 1,100 four-minute dates were recorded; **991** made it into the corpus and **946** had complete audio, transcript and survey data and were used. Both the speaker and the listener rated the speaker's flirtatiousness. — [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1035.pdf)
- **Outcome:** Self-reported intent to flirt, and partner-perceived flirting.
- **Method:** Prosodic, dialogue and lexical features. An **autoencoder compresses sparse lexical features into 30 features**. — [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1035.pdf)
- **Headline result:** The task is binary classification of each conversation side, with **chance at 50%**. Accuracy for **flirt intention** was **69.0% for men and 71.5% for women**. Accuracy for **flirt perception** was **79.5% for men and 68.0% for women**, up from 71% and 60% in Jurafsky et al. 2009. The gain came from the autoencoder features and an RBF-kernel SVM. The system also beat the human interlocutors (Table 6). "Humans are very poor perceivers of intended flirtatiousness, instead often projecting their own intended behavior onto their interlocutors." — [PDF](https://aclanthology.org/D09-1035.pdf)
- **Limitations:** Small training set, as the authors themselves note in the abstract. The labels are self- and partner ratings, not relationship formation.
- **Data availability:** not verified for this paper.

#### 4. Ranganath, Jurafsky & McFarland (2013), *Computer Speech & Language* [V-fulltext]
- **Citation:** Ranganath R, Jurafsky D, McFarland DA. (2013). Detecting friendly, flirtatious, awkward, and assertive speech in speed-dates. *Computer Speech & Language*, 27(1), 89–115. DOI 10.1016/j.csl.2012.01.005. — [Author-hosted PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
- **Data:** The SpeedDate corpus comes from 3 speed-dating sessions with graduate students at a US private university in 2005. It has about **1,100 four-minute dates**, about **60 h of speech** and about **800,000 transcribed words**. Analyses used **946 clean dates** with complete audio and transcripts (mean **812 words** and **93 turns** per date). Each participant rated self and partner on each stance on a 1–10 scale. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
- **Outcome:** Binary detection of the top 10% against the bottom 10% of each stance (flirtatious, friendly, awkward, assertive), both self-labeled and partner-labeled. For example, self-reported male flirtation had 174 examples. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
- **Method:** An SVM and L1-regularized logistic regression on lexical, prosodic and dialogue features, using 30 runs of 5-fold CV. A speaker-independent test trains on 2 events and tests on the third. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
- **Headline results:**
  - Up to **75% accuracy** on previously seen speakers (50% baseline), and up to **59%** on new speakers (48% baseline).
  - Mean SVM accuracy was **0.69** for top against bottom decile and **0.62** for top against bottom quartile.
  - The feature analysis linked flirtation to joint focus on the woman as the target of the conversation, and awkwardness to lower speaker involvement.
  - Speakers tend to give their partner the same stance rating they give themselves.

  — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
- **Limitations:** A single elite-university graduate sample from 2005, heterosexual speed dating only. Accuracy drops substantially on unseen speakers. Extreme-group (decile) labels inflate accuracy.
- **Data availability:** not verified. I did not see a public release statement in the PDF.

#### 5. McFarland, Jurafsky & Rawlings (2013), *American Journal of Sociology* [V-fulltext, partial]
- **Citation:** McFarland DA, Jurafsky D, Rawlings C. (2013). Making the Connection: Social Bonding in Courtship Situations. *American Journal of Sociology*, 118(6), 1596–1649. The volume, issue and pages come from a search summary; the PDF header's version is garbled and was not verified. The JSTOR stable URL 10.1086/670240 was verified in the PDF and is probably also the DOI. — [Author-hosted PDF (JSTOR copy)](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)
- **Data:** The same SpeedDate corpus, with **947 dates** that had audio and transcripts. Missing ratings were handled by multiple imputation. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)
- **Outcome:** Each person's 1–10 rating of having **"clicked"** with the partner (the main outcome), plus willingness to date. The paper argues that clicking mediates the speech-to-willingness link. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)
- **Method:** An actor–partner interdependence model (dyadic regression). Features are 6 prosodic factors (which explain 85% of the variance in 18 acoustic features), laughter, function-word mimicry (193 function words), appreciations, sympathy, interruptions, questions and hedges, with controls for traits and homophily. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)
- **Key finding:** Bonding happens "when females are the point of focus and engaged in the conversation and males demonstrate alignment with and understanding of the female", a "reciprocal asymmetrical performance". — [PDF abstract](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf). Press coverage adds that women reported connecting with men who used appreciative or sympathetic language, and that frequent questions were associated with worse dates. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed.stanford.edu/node/1819) (secondary)
- **Headline numbers:** I did not extract the regression coefficients or variance explained (not verified).
- **Limitations:** Associational, not out-of-sample prediction. Same single-site 2005 sample as #4.
- **Data availability:** not verified.

#### 6. Huang & Hancock (2022), *Journal of Language and Social Psychology* [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Huang SA, Hancock JT. (2022; online 17 Dec 2021). Will You Go on a Date with Me? Predicting First Dates from Linguistic Traces in Online Dating Messages. *Journal of Language and Social Psychology*, 41(4), 371–395. DOI 10.1177/0261927X211066612. — [Crossref abstract via DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X211066612); [Stanford SML page](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/hancock-jt/will-you-go-date-me-predicting-first-dates-linguistic-traces-online-dating)
- **Data:** Online dating message exchanges in **two studies**. The second is a new hold-out dataset for testing the hypotheses. The platform and sample sizes were **not verified**: they are not on the abstract pages I opened. — [Stanford SML](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/hancock-jt/will-you-go-date-me-predicting-first-dates-linguistic-traces-online-dating)
- **Outcome:** Whether a **first date took place**.
- **Method:** According to a secondary reading-notes page, LIWC-type features were grouped into five processes, and the analysis used t-tests with FDR correction and then logistic regression. The features included word count, words per sentence, pronouns ("we", "you", "I"), positive and negative emotion, exclamation marks, negations, interrogatives, insight, cause, tentativeness and certainty. — [Reading notes (secondary)](https://yikun-chi.gitbook.io/notes/paper-reading-notes/relationships/will-you-go-on-a-date-with-me-predicting-first-dates-from-linguistic-traces-in-online-dating-messag)
- **Headline result:** Study 1 identified **five interpersonal processes** tied to first-date outcomes: investment, interdependence, emotional dynamics, decision-making and coordination. Study 2 confirmed that linguistic features "can be used to predict above chance the likelihood of going on a first date". **The accuracy or AUC values were not verified.** — [Stanford SML](https://sml.stanford.edu/publications/hancock-jt/will-you-go-date-me-predicting-first-dates-linguistic-traces-online-dating)
- **Data availability:** SAGE hosts a figshare collection for the article, but its contents (supplement or data) were not verified. — [SAGE figshare](https://sage.figshare.com/collections/Will_You_Go_on_a_Date_with_Me_Predicting_First_Dates_from_Linguistic_Traces_in_Online_Dating_Messages/5766819)
- **Relevance:** This is the most direct modern analogue of "predict a relationship outcome from the conversation between two people" in online dating.

#### 7. Schoendienst & Dang-Xuan (2011), PACIS [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Schoendienst V, Dang-Xuan L. (2011). The Role of Linguistic Properties in Online Dating Communication – A Large-Scale Study of Contact Initiation Messages. *PACIS 2011 Proceedings*, paper 169. — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2011/169)
- **Data:** **167,276 initial messages** from an online dating platform. — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2011/169)
- **Outcome:** Whether the recipient **replies** to a first contact message.
- **Method:** LIWC features and regression analysis. — [AIS eLibrary](https://aisel.aisnet.org/pacis2011/169)
- **Headline result:** The abstract gives no direction of effects or coefficients (not verified). The full text returned HTTP 403. Directional findings are in Gaps.
- **Limitations:** Only the one-sided first message is modeled, not the exchange. Single unnamed platform. Conference paper.

#### 8. Garimella, Weber & Dal Cin (2014), SocInfo 2014 [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Garimella K, Weber I, Dal Cin S. (2014). From "I love you babe" to "leave me alone" – Romantic Relationship Breakups on Twitter. *Proc. 6th International Conference on Social Informatics (SocInfo 2014)*, Barcelona. arXiv:1409.5980. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.5980)
- **Data:** Public Twitter data for **661 couples** who broke up. Couples were identified through profile mentions, for example "@user2 is the best boyfriend ever". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.5980)
- **Outcome:** Breakup, and behavior before and after it.
- **Key findings:** Closeness before the relationship predicts closeness after it. **"Stonewalling" (ignoring the partner's messages) signals a pending breakup.** Post-breakup depression shows up in the tweets. "Batch un-friending" causes sudden drops of **15–20 followers and friends**. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.5980)
- **Method and accuracy:** The abstract reports no prediction accuracy (not verified).
- **Limitations:** Public tweets only. Couples are self-identified through profiles, which introduces selection bias. Descriptive rather than predictive modeling, as far as the abstract shows.

#### 9. Seraj, Blackburn & Pennebaker (2021), *PNAS* [V-abstract]
- **Citation:** Seraj S, Blackburn KG, Pennebaker JW. (2021). Language left behind on social media exposes the emotional and cognitive costs of a romantic breakup. *PNAS*, 118, e2017154118 (issue number not verified). DOI 10.1073/pnas.2017154118. PMCID PMC7896325. The abstract was read via the Europe PMC API; the PNAS page returned 403, and I did not open the PMC page. — [PMC (open access, not opened)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7896325/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2017154118)
- **Data:** **1,027,541 posts** from **6,803 Reddit users** who posted about a breakup, covering 2 years around the breakup. Replication samples: divorce (**n = 5,144**; 1,109,867 posts) and other upheavals (**n = 51,357**; 11,081,882 posts). — [abstract via Europe PMC API; PMC page](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7896325/)
- **Outcome:** Timing of the breakup (a language trajectory), and adjustment one year later.
- **Method:** LIWC time series (I-words, we-words, cognitive-processing words, the Analytic summary score).
- **Headline result:** Language markers of an impending breakup appear **3 months before** the event, peak in the week of the breakup and return to baseline about **6 months** later. I-words, we-words and cognitive-processing words rise, and analytic thinking falls. The pattern holds even in subreddits unrelated to relationships. People who posted about the breakup for longer were less well adjusted a year later. — [abstract via Europe PMC API; PMC page](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7896325/)
- **Limitations (important for scope):** These are posts from **one individual**, not language exchanged between the two partners. The breakup is self-disclosed, so there is selection bias. There is no classifier accuracy. The study is useful as evidence that a breakup signal precedes the event in text.
- **Data availability:** not verified. The open-access PMC page exists.

#### 10. Brinberg & Ram (2021), *Journal of Communication* [V-fulltext]
- **Citation:** Brinberg M, Ram N. (2021). Do New Romantic Couples Use More Similar Language Over Time? Evidence from Intensive Longitudinal Text Messages. *Journal of Communication*, 71(3), 454–477. DOI 10.1093/joc/jqab012. PMCID PMC8315721. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Data:** **41 college-age couples** (82 people, aged 18–23; 38 heterosexual and 3 same-sex couples), recruited Dec 2018 to Dec 2019. The couples **donated iPhone iMessage histories**, exported with iMessageAnalyzer. The corpus has **1,045,340 messages** over 6,452 days, and **1,001,454 messages** were analyzed. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Method:** Daily LSM (9 LIWC function-word categories), LSA semantic similarity and bag-of-words cosine similarity, modeled with nonlinear exponential growth models (nlme). — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Headline result:** Alignment rose exponentially toward an asymptote on all three metrics (all p < .001). The change to the asymptote was 0.03 for LSM, 0.06 for LSA and 0.08 for cosine. Most of the increase happened **before** couples reported "becoming a couple". — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Outcome prediction:** **None.** The study did not test whether alignment predicts satisfaction or breakup, and the authors list this as future work. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Limitations:** Convenience sample. Incomplete early logs. Text messages only. LIWC misses misspellings. De-identification placeholders may inflate alignment.
- **Data availability:** No public data. Raw messages were kept on secure servers accessible only to the study team. — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8315721)
- **Relevance:** This is the template for data-donation designs (donated message logs from both partners). A dataset like this could support outcome prediction.

#### 11. Joel, Eastwick & Finkel (2017), *Psychological Science* [V-abstract]: a contrast or baseline study, not language-based
- **Citation:** Joel S, Eastwick PW, Finkel EJ. (2017). Is Romantic Desire Predictable? Machine Learning Applied to Initial Romantic Attraction. *Psychological Science*, 28, 1478–1489 (issue number not verified). DOI 10.1177/0956797617714580. PMID 28853645. Abstract read via the Europe PMC API. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617714580)
- **Data:** Two speed-dating studies in which participants completed **more than 100 self-report trait and preference measures** before 4-minute speed dates. — [DOI / abstract via Europe PMC API](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617714580)
- **Method:** Random forests.
- **Headline result:** Random forests predicted **4–18% of actor variance** and **7–27% of partner variance**, but **could not predict relationship (dyad-specific) variance** from any combination of pre-date traits and preferences. — [DOI / abstract via Europe PMC API](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617714580)
- **Why include it:** This is a key negative result. Self-reports collected before people meet cannot predict who will like whom specifically. That motivates using the conversation itself as the predictor (#1, #3–#6). Note that it uses **no language data**.

#### 12. Schade, Sandberg, Bean, Busby & Coyne (2013) [V-biblio]: self-reported texting, not message content
- **Citation:** Schade LC, Sandberg J, Bean R, Busby D, Coyne S. (2013). Using Technology to Connect in Romantic Relationships: Effects on Attachment, Relationship Satisfaction, and Stability in Emerging Adults. *Journal of Couple & Relationship Therapy*, 12(4), 314–338. DOI 10.1080/15332691.2013.836051. Metadata confirmed via Crossref. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/15332691.2013.836051); [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/2343)
- **Data and findings:** not verified from any page I opened. A search-engine summary (not the pages themselves) attributed the following to the BYU ScholarsArchive abstract and BYU News coverage. N = 276 emerging adults aged 18–25 in committed relationships, with self-reported texting behaviors, **not actual messages**. Affectionate texting was linked to higher attachment. Hurtful texting by men was linked to lower satisfaction and stability. Men's texting frequency was negatively associated with satisfaction and stability. Women's use of texts to apologize or settle differences was linked to lower relationship quality. Confirm all of this before citing.
- **Limitations:** Correlational and self-report only. No text analysis.

### Inferences
- **Strongest dyadic-language prediction evidence:** Ireland et al. 2011 (LSM, with odds ratios), Ranganath et al. 2009/2013 (classifier accuracy) and Huang & Hancock 2022 (first dates, above chance). Seraj 2021 and Garimella 2014 add evidence that a breakup is foreshadowed in text, but at the individual or social-media level.
- **Predictability ceiling:** effect sizes are modest. Accuracy on unseen speakers is only about 59% against a 48% baseline (Ranganath 2013). Together with Joel et al. 2017, this suggests that dyad-specific compatibility is hard to predict. Conversation data does beat pre-meeting traits, but only moderately.
- **Possible double counting:** the 86-couple IM sample probably underlies both Slatcher & Pennebaker 2006 and Ireland et al. 2011 Study 2. A review should not treat these as independent replications.
- **Shared corpus:** the SpeedDate corpus (Stanford, 2005) underlies Ranganath 2009, Ranganath 2013 and McFarland 2013, so these three are also not independent samples.
- **Recent work is thin:** the field has moved to data donation (iMessage, WhatsApp; Brinberg & Ram 2021), but I found no peer-reviewed study that uses donated couple chat logs with modern ML or LLMs to predict breakup or satisfaction out of sample. Commercial "AI chat analyzer" sites claim this ability, but they are marketing and not evidence.

### Gaps
- **Huang & Hancock 2022:** the platform, N conversations and prediction accuracy were not verified (full text paywalled).
- **Schoendienst & Dang-Xuan 2011:** the coefficients were not verified (full text 403). A web-search summary, not a page I opened, claimed three findings. Messages with fewer self-references were more likely to get a reply. Gender moderated how recipients responded to linguistic traits. Message length significantly affected men's decisions to reply. Treat all three as unverified.
- **McFarland et al. 2013:** I did not extract specific coefficients or model fit.
- **Brody & Peña 2015 and Ohadi et al. 2018:** sample sizes, methods and numbers are unverified (publisher 403).
- **Data availability:** none of the verified papers states a public data release that I could confirm. The SpeedDate corpus's availability is unknown.
- **2022–2025 ML or LLM breakup and satisfaction prediction from chat logs:** two targeted searches found no peer-reviewed study.
